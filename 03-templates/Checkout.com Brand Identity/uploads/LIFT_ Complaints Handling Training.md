# **MODULE 1: Understanding Complaints & Tone**

### **What Is a Complaint?**

A complaint is when a customer tells us they’re unhappy with our service-  whether it's a strongly worded email, a politely raised concern, or a formal letter. At Checkout.com, complaints carry extra weight because:

* Our merchants' revenue depends directly on their payments working. A processing outage or a settlement delay isn't an inconvenience \- it's a commercial crisis.  
* Our contacts are often under pressure from their own finance directors, boards, or end customers.  
* In payments, trust is everything. A poorly handled complaint can accelerate a decision to move to a competitor PSP.  
* Merchants tend to be commercially and technically sophisticated \- they will notice vague or evasive responses immediately.


### **Why Tone Matters More in Writing**

Unlike a phone call, written responses are permanent. They can be:

* Forwarded to a client's senior leadership  
* Used as evidence in a dispute  
* Shared internally across their business

This means every word counts. Your tone needs to strike the right balance:

| Avoid | Aim for |
| ----- | ----- |
| Defensive | Accountable |
| Over-apologetic | Measured and sincere |
| Corporate/robotic | Professional but human |
| Dismissive | Validating |
| Vague | Clear and specific |

### **The Emotional Reality of Merchant Complaints**

Even in commercial relationships, complaints are emotional. A merchant raising a complaint may be:

* Facing pressure from their own board about conversion rates or revenue loss  
* Embarrassed in front of their own customers because their checkout is failing  
* Worried about regulatory or compliance exposure if a payout issue isn't resolved  
* Frustrated after raising a technical issue that hasn't been fixed despite escalation

Acknowledge the commercial and operational impact \- not just the technical problem.

---

## **🛠️ Quick Reference — Tone Dos and Don'ts**

**DO say:**

* "I understand this is directly affecting your ability to accept payments, and that's something we take seriously..."  
* "Thank you for raising this \- I want to make sure we get to the bottom of it..."  
* "I can see how much disruption this settlement delay has caused to your cash flow..."

**DON'T say:**

* "As per my previous email..." *(passive-aggressive)*  
* "Unfortunately there is nothing we can do at this stage..." *(defeatist)*  
* "This is not something that would normally happen on our platform..." *(defensive and dismissive)*  
* "I apologise for any inconvenience caused..." *(hollow \- payments issues are never just 'inconvenient')*

## **✏️ Exercise 1A: Tone Rewrite**

## *"Thank you for your email. We are sorry to hear you are unhappy. As per our platform terms, Checkout.com is not liable for authorisation rate fluctuations caused by issuer behaviour. We hope this clarifies the matter."*

## **Consider:** What is missing? What would a merchant feel reading this? How can you acknowledge the impact while still being factually accurate about where responsibility sits?

A merchant watching their auth rate drop and losing revenue will feel completely abandoned. It also assumes the problem is entirely issuer-side without investigating \- which may not be true.

* ## Don’t deflect without evidence

* ## Commit to investigation with a specific timeframe

# **MODULE 2: Structure & Format of Written Responses**

### **The AIR Framework**

A strong complaint response follows a clear structure. Use the **AIR** framework:

**A \- Acknowledge** Open by recognising the complaint and its impact. Do this before anything else, never jump straight to explanation or solution.

**I \- Investigate & Inform** Explain what happened (if known), what you've looked into, and what the facts are. Be honest. If you don't have all the answers yet, say so and give a timeline.

**R \- Resolve** Set out clearly what you are going to do, by when, and what the client can expect next. If appropriate, include what steps you're taking to prevent recurrence.

### **Response Structure in Practice**

**Opening**

* Acknowledge receipt and thank them for raising it  
* Validate the impact

**Middle**

* Summarise your understanding of the complaint (shows you've read it carefully)  
* Explain what happened (factually, without blame-shifting)  
* Outline what you've done or are doing to investigate

**Close**

* State the resolution or next steps clearly  
* Give a specific timeframe  
* Invite further contact with a named person

### **Formatting Tips for Written B2B Complaints**

* **Keep paragraphs short** \- long blocks of text feel like you're burying the point  
* **Use the client's name** \- not "Dear Customer"  
* **Avoid jargon and acronyms** \- their team may forward this internally  
* **Sign off with a name** \- not just "Merchant Care"  
* **Match their formality level** \- if they wrote formally, respond formally

---

## **🛠️ Quick Reference: AIR Framework Checklist**

Before sending any complaint response, check:

* \[ \] Does the opening acknowledge the complaint *and* its impact?  
* \[ \] Have I summarised what they raised? (Shows I read it)  
* \[ \] Have I explained what happened without being defensive?  
* \[ \] Is my resolution clear and specific?  
* \[ \] Have I given a timeframe?  
* \[ \] Is the tone professional but human?  
* \[ \] Have I used their name and signed with mine?  
* \[ \] Would I be comfortable if this was shared with their CEO?

# **MODULE 3: Handling Difficult or Escalated Complaints**

### **What Makes a Complaint Get Escalated?**

A complaint becomes escalated when:

* It has been raised before and not resolved satisfactorily  
* The client has involved senior stakeholders on their side  
* There is a financial claim or commercial consequence attached  
* The language is threatening, legal, or formal in nature  
* The relationship is at risk

### **The Golden Rules for Escalated Written Complaints**

**1\. Respond promptly, even if you don't have the answer** Silence makes things worse. A brief holding response buys time and shows respect:

*"I've received your email and I'm treating it as a priority. I will come back to you with a full response by \[specific date/time\]."*

**2\. Elevate the signer** If the original complaint was handled by a junior team member, an escalated response should come from a manager or senior contact. This signals that you're taking it seriously.

**3\. Never be defensive in writing** Even if the client is wrong, a defensive response will escalate things further and creates a paper trail that reflects poorly on your business.

**4\. Separate fact from feeling** Acknowledge the emotional content first, then deal with the facts. Don't get drawn into an argument about who is right before you've validated how they feel.

**5\. Be clear about what you can and cannot do** False promises made under pressure are worse than being honest upfront. If there are limits to what you can offer, say so clearly and with empathy.

### **Managing Aggressive or Threatening Language**

Occasionally a business contact will use language that is aggressive or threatening. In writing:

* Do not mirror the tone  
* Do not ignore it \- acknowledge it briefly and redirect  
* Document the exchange internally  
* Know when to escalate internally to your manager or legal team

---

## **🛠️ Quick Reference — Escalation Response Guide**

| Situation | Suggested Action |
| ----- | ----- |
| Repeat complaint | Acknowledge the history explicitly \- don't pretend it's the first time |
| Senior stakeholder involved | Ensure a senior person signs the response |
| Financial claim mentioned | Do not admit liability \- acknowledge and refer to appropriate team |
| Legal language used | Escalate internally before responding |
| Threatening language | Respond calmly, document, escalate |
| Relationship at risk | Propose a direct conversation (call or meeting) in addition to written response |

# **MODULE 4: Regulatory & Compliance AwarenessWhy Compliance Matters in Complaints Handling**

* **Data protection:** Written complaints may contain personal data. Handle, store and share them appropriately under UK data protection and EU GDPR.  
* **Record keeping:** All complaints and responses should be logged. This protects both the client and Checkout.com  
* **Liability:** Written responses can be used as evidence, make sure they are factuall correct.  
* **Fairness:** Complaints must be handled consistently \- the same process should apply regardless of the size of the client account.

### **What to Log for Every Complaint**

* Date complaint received  
* Name and role of the contact  
* Nature of the complaint (summary)  
* Date and content of all responses  
* Resolution agreed  
* Date closed

### **Admitting Liability:  What to Avoid**

There is a difference between acknowledging an issue and admitting legal liability. You can say:

✅ *"I can see this has not met the standard you'd expect from us."* ✅ *"We take full responsibility for addressing this promptly."*

But avoid: ❌ *"We accept that this was entirely our fault."* ❌ *"We acknowledge we are in breach of our contract."*

If a complaint has legal or financial implications, always check with a manager before responding.

# Threatening Language

# **Handling Threatening Language in Written Complaints**

## Supplementary Guide 

---

## **What Counts as Threatening Language?**

Threatening language in merchant complaints falls into three broad categories. It's important to recognise which type you're dealing with, because the response differs.

| Type | What It Looks Like | What It Usually Means |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Commercial threats** | "We will move our processing to another PSP", "I'll be raising this with our board" | Frustration \+ leverage. Merchant wants to feel heard and see action. |
| **Regulatory threats** | "I will be contacting the FCA", "We will report this to the relevant authorities" | Serious frustration, possibly genuine intent. Must be escalated before responding. |
| **Legal threats** | "Our solicitors will be in touch", "We will be pursuing this as a breach of contract" | High stakes. Must be escalated to Legal. |

**The Three-Step Redirect**

When a complaint contains threatening language, follow this structure in your written response:

### **Step 1\. Acknowledge, don't react**

Name the frustration without repeating or amplifying the threat. Do not ignore it, that signals you haven't read the email properly. But do not engage with the threat itself.

✅ *"I can hear how frustrated you are, and understand why you’ll be taking this route given what you’ve described"* 

❌ *"I note your comments regarding the FCA"*  cold and defensive 

❌ *"There is no need to involve solicitors at this stage"*  escalatory

### **Step 2\. Take personal ownership**

The threat is usually a signal that the merchant doesn't believe anyone is going to fix the problem. The antidote is a named individual visibly taking responsibility.

✅ *"I'm taking personal ownership of this and I don't want it going anywhere else until we've had the chance to resolve it properly."* 

❌ *"Your complaint has been escalated to the relevant team"*  impersonal and vague

### **Step 3\. Move forward, not backward**

Redirect the energy toward resolution and propose a specific next step.

✅ *"Can we get on a call today? I'd rather talk this through directly than keep going back and forth in writing."* 

❌ *"Please be assured we are investigating and will revert in due course"*  no momentum, no ownership

## **Worked Examples**

### **Scenario A: Regulatory Threat**

**Incoming message:**

*"Our settlement has now been held for eleven days with no adequate explanation. This is our operational revenue and we cannot continue to function without it. If we do not receive our funds and a full written explanation by close of business tomorrow, we will be filing a formal complaint with the FCA and instructing our legal team."*

⚠️ **Before responding:** This message references both the FCA and legal action. Do not send a response without escalating to your manager and, if necessary, Checkout.com's legal or compliance team first.

**❌ Bad response:**

*"Dear \[Name\], Thank you for your email. We understand your frustration and are working to resolve the settlement hold as quickly as possible. We hope to have an update for you soon."*

**Why it fails:** An eleven-day settlement hold is a serious issue. "As quickly as possible" and "hope to have an update soon" are completely inadequate responses to a merchant threatening FCA involvement. This response is likely to accelerate the complaint rather than contain it.

**✅ Good response (post-escalation approval):**

*"Dear \[Name\], Thank you for your email and firstly allow me to say how sorry I am you’ve waited for this length of time.*

*An eleven-day hold on your operational revenue is not something I'm treating lightly, and I understand the position this has put your business in. I'm sorry it has reached this point.*

*I have escalated this within Checkout.com as an urgent priority. I am committed to providing you with a full written explanation of the hold and a confirmed release timeline by \[specific time\]  that is a firm commitment, not a placeholder.”*

**Why it works:** It acknowledges the severity without admitting liability, makes a specific and firm commitment, and offers a direct conversation, all without engaging with the FCA threat directly or dismissing it.

---

### **Scenario B: Aggressive Personal Language**

**Incoming message:**

*"Your support team is completely useless and I am sick of being fobbed off with template responses. Whoever is reading this clearly doesn't care about our business. Sort this out or I will make sure everyone in our network knows how Checkout.com treats its merchants."*

**❌ Bad response:**

*"Dear \[Name\], Thank you for your email. We are sorry you feel this way. We do not tolerate abuse of our staff. We will be in touch with an update in due course."*

**Why it fails:** "We do not tolerate abuse of our staff" while potentially valid, will immediately inflame the situation further. "Sorry you feel this way" is dismissive. The merchant has already said they feel fobbed off with template responses, and this response is exactly that.

**✅ Good response:**

*"Dear \[Name\], I've read your email carefully and I'm not going to respond with a template, you've made clear that hasn't worked, and I can understand why that's been frustrating.*

*I don't know yet exactly where we've let you down, but I'm looking through the case now. Can you give me 20 minutes on a call today? I want to hear directly from you what's happened.”*

**Why it works:** It directly addresses the merchant's specific frustration (template responses), doesn't mirror the aggressive tone or lecture them about their language, and immediately proposes a way out of the written exchange \- which is where the frustration is building.

---

## **What to Do After Sending**

Regardless of which type of threat you've received:

1. **Log the complaint immediately** \- include the specific threatening language used and the time it was received.  
2. **Notify your manager** \- even if you've already responded appropriately to a commercial threat, your manager should know.  
3. **Do not close the complaint** \- keep it open until there is a confirmed resolution and the merchant has acknowledged it.  
4. **Follow up when you said you would** \- a missed callback or update after a threatening complaint will almost certainly lead to escalation.  
5. **Document your response rationale** \- note why you responded as you did, particularly if you chose not to address the threatening language directly.

---

## **What Never to Do**

* ❌ Ignore the threat and respond as if it wasn't there  
* ❌ Threaten back or reference consequences for the merchant  
* ❌ Make commitments you haven't had authorised (especially compensation or liability)  
* ❌ Respond to a legal or FCA threat without management sign-off  
* ❌ Use the merchant's aggressive language as a reason to deprioritise their complaint  
* ❌ Copy in multiple people without thinking \- wider distribution can escalate rather than resolve
